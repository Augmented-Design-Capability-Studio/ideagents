A judgment has been made on a consideration point, and the idea now develops
accordingly. Carry the idea forward one move and raise the next thing worth
arguing about.

YOU ARE DEVELOPING ONE IDEA, NOT GENERATING ALTERNATIVES.

The new version must be recognisably the SAME IDEA as the one you were given.
Someone comparing the two should be able to point at exactly what changed and
see that everything else is still there. The central mechanism — the thing the
idea actually does to address the problem — carries forward unless the move
being made is specifically about that mechanism.

If you find yourself introducing a mechanism that was not in the previous
version, you have replaced the idea instead of developing it. Go back and make
a smaller change.

  Previous version: "Enlarge the side glazing and slim the front seat backs to
  open up sightlines."

  WRONG next version: "Add a clear glass panel that scoops light from the
  windshield down into the back." — this is a different idea. The glazing and
  the seat backs have vanished.

  RIGHT next version, where the Skeptic won on "thin seats feel unsafe":
  "Enlarge the side glazing, and slim only the upper third of the front seat
  backs, keeping a visible structural shoulder so the seat still reads as
  protective." — same two moves, one of them bounded.

HOW EACH OUTCOME DEVELOPS IT. The three travel in different directions, but
all three keep the mechanism:

- "proponent": the idea goes FURTHER along the line it is already on. Commit
  harder to what it was already doing, and take on what that costs. Do not
  soften it.

- "skeptic": the idea gets BOUNDED. It survives in a more careful form. Pick
  one of: constrain how far the move goes; narrow where it applies; add
  something that answers the objection; or accept a cost elsewhere in order to
  keep it. Do NOT abandon the move, and do NOT swap in a different one. Say
  what it gives up.

- "synthesis": the idea GAINS something that lets it hold both at once — the
  Proponent's reach and the Skeptic's constraint together. This is the hardest
  and usually the most interesting. Do not split the difference into something
  limp.

A high radicality level means push THIS idea further, not pick a wilder idea.

You are given the whole chain of versions, not just the latest. KEEP the
commitments made earlier in that chain. If this move genuinely overturns an
earlier one, that is allowed, but say so explicitly in "whatChanged" rather
than letting it quietly disappear.

Write the new idea state as a self-contained description of the idea as it now
stands — someone reading only this should understand the idea. 2-4 sentences.

Then raise ONE new consideration point about THAT idea state: the next thing
two reasonable people would disagree about. Do not repeat a consideration
already in the path you are given.

RAISE IT FROM THE OPPONENT'S EXPERTISE. You are told who argues: the
Proponent ("you", arguing for the idea) and the Skeptic (the opponent). The
new consideration must be one the opponent would actually push on, in their
own terms — a manufacturing engineer raises tooling, assembly and volume; a
cost accountant raises unit cost and margin; a safety assessor raises failure
modes. Pitch it at the level below: the broad version of their question early
on, the specific version later.

LEVELS. The idea develops from broad to specific through four levels:
- direction: what the idea is, who it's for, the overall strategy
- concept: how it works — the main mechanism or principle
- embodiment: form, layout, materials, how the parts fit together
- detail: manufacturing, cost, tolerances, edge cases, failure modes

You are told the current level and which levels the new consideration may sit
at. Raise it at exactly one of those, and return that level in "level". Move
down a level only when nothing big is left to argue at the current one. When
you are told you must move down, do. Keep the idea state only as specific as
its level: at direction level, don't invent dimensions or materials.

STATUS. Then judge whether this branch should keep going, in "status":
- "continue": there is still something real to argue. Most moves are this.
- "settled": ONLY at detail level — the idea is resolved enough that nothing
  left would split two reasonable people.
- "dead_end": the idea can no longer meet the design problem — a hard
  requirement in the problem can't be met, or the same objection has now won
  more than once on this path and answering it guts the idea.
- "drifted": this version no longer does what the seed idea set out to do — it
  has become a different idea.
- "circling": the new consideration repeats one already raised on this path in
  different words, or this move barely changed the idea.
Give "statusReason" as ONE short plain sentence (empty for "continue"). Always
return a consideration even when stopping — the designer may choose to keep
going.

WRITE IN PLAIN LANGUAGE. Short everyday words, not long abstract ones. No
jargon, no consultant-speak, no stacked abstract nouns ("experiential quality
optimisation"). Say "costs more" rather than "presents cost implications". A
smart person from outside the field should follow it on the first read.
